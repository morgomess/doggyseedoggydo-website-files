Why Is My Dog's Skin So Dry in Winter? (Heating, Bath Habits and the Easy Fixes at Home)

Nov 11, 2026•5 min read•Dog Health
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

The heat kicks on in November and suddenly your dog is scratching, your couch is covered in flakes, and their coat looks about as vibrant as a dead houseplant. You have not done anything wrong. Winter dry skin in dogs is genuinely common, and most of the time the cause is sitting right there on your thermostat.

Here is what is actually going on, what you can do about it at home, and the signs that tell you it is time to call your vet.

What winter does to your dog's skin

Cold air holds less moisture than warm air. That is just physics. So when the temperature drops outside and your furnace cranks up inside, you get a double hit of dry air working against your dog's skin and coat all day and all night.

Indoor heating is the big one. Forced air systems, whether gas, electric or heat pump, pull moisture out of the air inside your home. Humidity levels in a heated house in winter can drop well below what is comfortable for skin, human or canine. Your dog lives at floor level, close to the vents, and they cannot step outside the problem the way you can by moisturizing your hands or taking a warm shower.

The result is skin that loses moisture faster than it can replace it. You see that as flaking, dullness, extra scratching, and sometimes a coat that feels rougher than usual.

The bath habit that makes it worse

Here is where well-meaning owners sometimes make things harder for their dogs. If you are bathing more frequently in winter, maybe because your dog is tracking in mud or you are just noticing the smell more, and you are using a shampoo that is not formulated for dogs, you could be stripping the natural oils right out of their coat.

Dog skin has a different pH from human skin. Human shampoos, including baby shampoos, are not a great match. Even some dog shampoos can be too harsh if used too often. Those natural coat oils are what keep skin moisturized and protected. Strip them out repeatedly and the dryness gets worse, not better.

A good rule of thumb for winter: bathe less, not more, and use a gentle, dog-specific shampoo every time.

How to tell if it is dry skin

Dry skin in dogs tends to show up as a combination of these things:

  • Visible flakes on the coat or on dark surfaces where your dog has been lying
  • Scratching or rubbing against furniture more than usual
  • A dull, rough-feeling coat instead of a soft or shiny one
  • Mildly red skin, usually visible on the belly or inside the legs

These signs showing up after the heat comes on in the fall is a pretty reliable indicator that dry air is the culprit. That said, not every itch and flake is a heating problem, and knowing when to look further matters.

Do not write off fleas because it is cold

Fleas do not disappear in winter. They slow down outdoors below about 55 degrees Fahrenheit, but inside your heated home they are perfectly comfortable all year round. If your dog was exposed in the fall before the cold really set in, fleas can keep cycling through your home through winter without you realizing it.

So if the scratching is intense, if you spot anything that looks like tiny dark specks in the coat, or if the itching seems focused around the base of the tail, rule fleas out before you assume it is purely a dry skin issue. Your vet or a good flea comb will tell you quickly.

The easy fixes you can do at home

Most winter dry skin responds well to a few straightforward changes. None of these require a big spend or any guesswork.

Get a humidifier running. A basic cool mist humidifier in the room where your dog sleeps makes a real difference. You are not trying to recreate a tropical climate, just take the edge off the dry heated air. Your skin will thank you too.

Brush regularly. This is one of the most underrated things you can do. Brushing distributes the natural oils already in your dog's coat from root to tip, which is exactly where dry skin needs them. It also helps you keep an eye on the skin underneath so you notice changes early.

Cut back on baths. In winter, most dogs do not need bathing as often as they might in muddy spring or summer. When you do bathe, use a gentle, moisturizing shampoo made for dogs and rinse thoroughly so no product residue sits on the skin.

Check your water temperature. Very hot water strips oils faster than lukewarm water. Keep bath water comfortably warm, not hot.

Food and omega-3s. A good quality diet that includes omega-3 fatty acids, found in fish and fish oil, supports skin health from the inside out. If you are thinking about adding a supplement, that is a conversation worth having with your vet, who can advise on what is appropriate for your dog's size and health.

When to call your vet

Home fixes cover most cases of winter dry skin. But some signs tell you this has gone beyond what a humidifier and a gentle shampoo will solve. Call your vet if you see:

  • Constant, intense itching that is not easing up
  • Red, broken, or raw skin anywhere on the body
  • Sores, scabs, or patches of hair loss
  • Skin that looks thickened, or a coat coming out in significant amounts
  • Symptoms that started before the heat came on and do not match the seasonal pattern

Conditions like allergies, mange, ringworm, or hormonal issues can show up looking similar to dry skin. A vet can tell the difference and get your dog the right help. This post covers general home care for the common seasonal stuff. If something feels off or is getting worse, trust that instinct and get it checked.

The short version

Dry, flaky, itchy skin when the heating comes on is usually exactly what it looks like: a moisture problem caused by dry indoor air and sometimes a bath routine that is working against the coat. Run a humidifier, brush regularly, dial back bath frequency, use a gentle shampoo, and keep an eye on your dog's diet. That combination handles most cases.

And if the scratching is relentless, the skin is broken, or something just does not look right, call your vet. Because Google does not have a dog, but your vet does. 🐾
