Road Trips With Your Dog: Car Safety, Carsickness, and Long-Drive Survival

Aug 12, 2026•8 min read•Seasonal Safety
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

Labor Day weekend is coming. You've got a full tank, a destination, and a dog who starts drooling the moment you turn out of the driveway. This is the guide for you.

Whether your dog is a seasoned back-seat traveler or has never been further than the vet's parking lot, dog car travel tips that actually work come down to three things: keeping them safe, keeping their stomach settled, and keeping everyone sane across a few hundred miles. Let's get into it.

Your Dog Loose in the Back Seat Is a Projectile

Not trying to scare you. Just physics.

An unrestrained 60-pound dog in a 35 mph crash generates roughly 2,700 pounds of force. That is the kind of number crash-test engineers lose sleep over, and it is why dog road trip safety starts before you reverse out of the driveway.

You have two solid options.

A crash-tested harness clips into the seat belt buckle and keeps your dog seated in the back. Not all harnesses are equal here. Look for one that has gone through independent crash testing, not just marketing copy about being "safety certified." The Center for Pet Safety has tested specific models if you want to dig into the data.

A secured travel crate is the other route. It needs to be anchored so it cannot launch forward in a stop. A crate rattling around in a cargo area is not safer than a harness, it is just a different kind of projectile. Strap it down or wedge it tight against the seat.

Cargo barriers are fine for keeping a dog out of the front seat but they do not protect the dog in a real impact. Think of them as manners management, not safety gear.

One more note: several states have laws about transporting unsecured animals in a vehicle, and others are updating theirs. It is worth a quick search for your state before you go.

Why Your Dog Gets Carsick (and What Actually Helps)

Carsickness in dogs is mostly an inner ear problem, especially in younger dogs whose vestibular system is still developing. The motion the body feels does not match what the eyes see, and that conflict produces nausea. Anxiety can layer on top and make it significantly worse.

Signs your dog is carsick: excessive drooling, yawning repeatedly, lip licking, whining, or vomiting. Some dogs go quiet and glassy-eyed before they go off like a garden hose.

Here is what genuinely helps with preventing dog carsickness.

  • Do not feed a big meal before a long drive. A light snack two to three hours before you leave is fine. A full bowl an hour before departure is asking for trouble.
  • Crack the windows. Fresh air and equalizing the air pressure inside the car can reduce nausea noticeably. It does not have to be a gale, just enough airflow to help.
  • Let your dog face forward if you can. Watching the road ahead is less disorienting than watching scenery blur past sideways.
  • Break the anxiety loop. A lot of car-hating dogs are not sick from motion, they are sick from stress. If your dog only rides in the car to go to the vet, they have made a very logical connection that cars equal bad things.

For dogs with persistent motion sickness, there are vet-approved medications including Cerenia, which is specifically licensed for motion sickness in dogs. That is a conversation for your vet before the trip, not a last-minute decision on a Friday afternoon. Ginger in small amounts has some anecdotal support and is generally safe, but it is not a substitute for a vet chat if your dog is genuinely struggling.

Building a Dog Who Doesn't Hate the Car

If your dog hits panic mode the moment they see the car keys, you are dealing with a negative association that has built up over time. You can change it, but it takes a few short sessions before a big trip, not the morning of.

  • Start by just sitting in the parked car with the engine off. Feed treats. Get out. That is the whole session.
  • Next time, engine on, still parked. Treats. Good things happen here.
  • Then a very short drive, end of the street and back, followed by something the dog loves. A walk, a game, their favorite chew. The car predicts good stuff now.

Do this a handful of times in the week before a big trip and most dogs shift noticeably. It is not a magic fix for severe anxiety but it works well for dogs who are just skeptical.

The Long-Drive Rhythm: Stops, Water, and Exercise

Dogs on long drives need more than a full water bowl at the destination. Here is a workable rhythm for a day's drive.

  • Stop every two to three hours at minimum. Stretch, sniff, bathroom break. Even ten minutes of walking around a rest area does a lot for a dog's stress levels and circulation.
  • Bring water from home. Some dogs get stomach upset from water with different mineral content. It sounds fussy but it is a real thing, especially for dogs already dealing with travel stress. A collapsible bowl takes up no space.
  • Bring something familiar. A dog's own blanket or a worn t-shirt from home in the crate or on the seat gives them a scent anchor. It is a small thing that makes a real difference.
  • Do not let your dog hang their head out at highway speed. The ear-flapping joy of it is real, but at 70 mph a piece of debris can cause a serious eye or ear injury. Slightly open is fine.

The One Rule You Cannot Skip: Never Leave a Dog in a Parked Car in Summer

A car parked in August sun reaches 120 degrees Fahrenheit inside within 20 minutes, even with the windows cracked. Dogs do not cool by sweating. They pant, and in a closed hot car they cannot pant fast enough to survive.

This is not a situation that allows for a quick coffee or a five-minute errand. If you cannot take your dog into a space with you and there is no one to stay with them in the running air-conditioned car, they wait at home for this leg of the trip.

What to Pack: A Short List

  • Water and a collapsible bowl
  • Enough food for the trip plus extra
  • Leash, collar with current ID tag
  • Recent vet records and vaccination proof, essential if you are crossing state lines or stopping at a dog-friendly hotel
  • Poop bags, paper towels, and a small bottle of enzymatic cleaner because optimism is not a strategy
  • A familiar blanket or toy
  • Any medications your dog takes regularly
  • Your vet's number and the number of an emergency vet near your destination

The Bottom Line

Dog car travel does not have to be a survival event. The dogs who travel well are usually the dogs whose owners took thirty minutes to think through the setup before they left. Secure them properly, manage the stomach situation, keep the stops coming, and never leave them in a hot car.

Your dog cannot read a map or ask for a rest stop. That part is on you, but you have got this. 🐾
