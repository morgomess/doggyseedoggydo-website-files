Fleas and Ticks in Late Summer: The Prevention Routine That Actually Works

Aug 26, 2026•7 min read•Health

Late August through October is not the time to ease up on parasite prevention. It is, annoyingly, the time ticks and fleas are at their worst. The air is warm, the grass is long, and your dog has no idea they just walked through a hitchhiker convention.

Good news: protecting your dog does not require an elaborate routine or a cabinet full of products. It requires consistency and knowing what to actually look for. That is what this post is about.

Where Ticks and Fleas Are Actually Hiding

You probably picture ticks lurking deep in dense forest. That picture is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Ticks hang out in the transitional zones: the strip of tall grass between your mowed lawn and the tree line, the leaf pile you have been meaning to rake, the shady underside of your deck, the edge of the trail where you cut through on your regular morning walk.

Fleas love shade and humidity. Think crawl spaces, the base of shrubs, spots where a stray cat has been resting, and any area that rarely dries out fully in the summer heat.

Your own backyard is often more of a source than you think. Keeping grass mowed short, clearing out leaf litter, and cutting back overgrown shrubs reduces the hangout spots available to both pests significantly.

The Post-Walk Check: Two Minutes, Done Right

The single most effective daily habit you can build costs nothing and takes about the length of a song on the radio. The trick is doing it by zone, not just running your hand vaguely over your dog's back.

Work through these spots every time your dog has been in grass, brush, or anywhere that is not concrete:

  • Ears and ear flap base. Ticks love the warm skin right at the attachment point. Part the fur and look, do not just feel.
  • Collar line. Ticks crawl upward and often stop here. Remove the collar occasionally for a proper look.
  • Armpits and groin. Soft, warm, hidden. Check both.
  • Between the toes. Easy to miss, especially on dogs with thick fur between their pads.
  • Tail base. This is also a prime flea zone. If your dog has been chewing or scratching at the tail base and rump, that is a signal worth paying attention to.
  • Under the chin and around the muzzle. Dogs who sniff through brush can pick up ticks on their face.

A tick that has just landed is small. A tick that has been feeding for a day or two is easier to find. Ideally you want to find them before they have had a chance to settle in, so the check right after the walk matters more than a check the next morning.

How to Remove a Tick Without Squeezing It

Use fine-tipped tweezers or a dedicated tick removal tool. Grip the tick as close to the skin as possible, right at the head, and pull upward with steady, even pressure. No twisting, no squeezing the body, no petroleum jelly, no burning it off.

Dispose of it in a sealed bag or jar, or flush it. Wash the bite area and your hands with soap and water.

If part of the tick remains in the skin, or if the bite area becomes inflamed or changes over the following days, that is when you stop reading blogs and call your vet.

The Flea Signs Owners Miss

Fleas are fast and hard to spot on a moving dog, especially on dark-coated breeds. What you are more likely to find first is evidence they leave behind.

  • Flea dirt. It looks exactly like coarse ground pepper scattered through the fur, particularly around the tail base, belly, and inner thighs. Here is how to confirm what you are seeing: scoop a little onto a damp white paper towel. Flea dirt turns reddish-brown because it is digested blood. Regular dirt stays dark or gray.
  • Hot spots and hair loss at the tail base and rump. Some dogs are allergic to flea saliva, and a single flea bite can trigger intense scratching, chewing, and skin inflammation. The damage looks disproportionate to the cause.
  • Restless behavior at night. Fleas are more active in the dark. If your dog is unusually unsettled at bedtime and you have ruled out other causes, fleas are worth checking for.

Here is the part that trips a lot of owners up: by the time you notice your dog scratching, the infestation is not new. The itch shows up after repeated exposure, and the flea population in your home has usually been building for weeks before the signs become obvious. Adult fleas on your dog are only a fraction of the total population. The eggs, larvae, and pupae are in your carpet, your dog's bedding, and the sofa.

The Honest Line on Year-Round Prevention

Flea and tick prevention is not something you buy in April, forget by June, and restart when you notice a problem. Late summer and fall are peak seasons, and in warmer parts of the US, fleas especially stay active well into winter. Year-round pest control for dogs and cats is not overcaution. It is just the reality of how these pests operate.

Talk to your vet about which preventative option fits your dog, your lifestyle, and your geography. Collars, topicals, and oral options all work differently, and there is no single answer that is right for every dog. What matters is that something is in place, used consistently, and not expired.

Do Indoor Pets Need Prevention Too?

Yes. Fleas come in on your clothing and shoes. Ticks can ride in on other pets or on you. And the pet who never leaves the house often does not have any natural exposure-based resistance to flea saliva, which can make a reaction worse when it eventually happens.

Indoor-only does not mean risk-free. It means lower risk, which is different.

Keep Your Home and Yard Part of the Routine

Vacuum frequently, especially along baseboards, under furniture, and anywhere your dog sleeps. Wash pet bedding on a hot cycle regularly. If you have a flea problem, vacuuming can help disrupt the life cycle, but it is not a substitute for treating the environment properly.

Outside: mow regularly, clear leaf piles, and trim back shrubs. Shade and humidity are what pests need. Reducing both in your yard reduces the population available to hitch a ride inside.

When to Call the Vet

This is the honest line. If you are finding multiple ticks regularly, if your dog's skin is inflamed or they are losing fur, if you suspect a flea infestation that has reached the home environment, or if you are simply not sure what you are looking at, that is what vets are for.

General information, including everything in this post, is a good starting point. It is not a substitute for professional advice when your specific dog needs it.

The two-minute check after every walk will not catch everything, but it will catch a lot. Build the habit now, while it is peak season, and keep it going when the weather cools. Ticks do not stop at the first frost. Neither should you. 🐾
