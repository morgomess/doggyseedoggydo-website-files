10 Signs Your Dog Is Trying to Tell You They're Sick

Mar 5, 2025•6 min read•Health

Dogs are wired to hide weakness. It's an instinct that served their ancestors well and serves your worry levels poorly, because by the time a dog "looks sick," they've often been unwell for a while. Here are the signals worth knowing before a small problem becomes an emergency.

Why dogs hide illness

In the wild, an animal that broadcasts pain becomes a target. That instinct didn't disappear when dogs moved onto our couches. Your dog will often carry on wagging and eating through discomfort that would have a human calling in sick. That's exactly why learning the subtle signs matters - you're reading a patient who's actively trying not to tell you anything.

The signs worth watching

1. Appetite changes. Both directions count. A dog suddenly uninterested in food, or one that's ravenous and losing weight anyway, is flagging something. One skipped meal isn't a crisis; a clear pattern is.

2. Drinking and urination shifts. Noticeably more thirst and more frequent urination can point to kidney issues, diabetes, or other endocrine problems. A dog suddenly drinking far more than usual deserves a vet conversation, not a wait-and-see.

3. Energy and engagement drops. Lethargy is vague but real. The dog who normally meets you at the door and now doesn't, who sleeps through cues that used to thrill them, is telling you something even if nothing else looks wrong.

4. Bathroom changes. Diarrhea, constipation, straining, blood, or a change in color or frequency all matter. A single soft stool happens. Persistent changes, or any blood, warrant attention.

5. Vomiting that doesn't quit. Occasional vomiting is common and often benign. Repeated vomiting, vomiting with lethargy, or an inability to keep water down moves into urgent territory.

6. Coat and skin changes. A dull coat, new bald patches, excessive scratching, or unusual lumps can reflect anything from allergies to thyroid problems. Run your hands over your dog regularly so you notice new bumps early.

7. Breathing changes. Panting at rest, labored breathing, or persistent coughing are never things to ignore. Breathing trouble can escalate fast.

8. Mobility and posture. Limping, stiffness after rest, reluctance on stairs, or a hunched posture often signal pain. Dogs rarely cry out; they just quietly stop doing things that hurt.

9. Bad breath and mouth changes. Genuinely foul breath isn't just "dog breath" - it can signal dental disease or, occasionally, metabolic problems. Drooling, pawing at the mouth, or dropping food are related flags.

10. Behavior and temperament shifts. A sudden change in personality - new irritability, hiding, clinginess, or aggression when touched in a certain spot - is one of the most reliable signs that something hurts. Pain changes behavior before it changes anything you can see.

When to call now versus watch

Call your vet immediately for: difficulty breathing, repeated vomiting or diarrhea, a distended or hard abdomen, collapse, suspected poisoning, inability to urinate, or any bleeding that won't stop. These don't get a wait-and-see window.

For milder, single-instance changes, a 24-hour watch is reasonable - but trust your read. You know your dog's normal better than anyone, and "something is off" from the person who lives with them is genuinely useful diagnostic information.

The bottom line

You're not trying to become a diagnostician. You're trying to notice change early, because early is where the cheap, easy fixes live. Know your dog's baseline - their normal energy, appetite, and habits - and let any meaningful drift from it be the thing that prompts a call. The dogs who do best are the ones whose people caught the quiet signals first.

This article is general information, not veterinary advice. When in doubt, call your vet.
