Should You Feed Your Dog More in Winter? (How to Tell by Body Condition, Not the Thermometer)

Nov 14, 2026•6 min read•Dog Nutrition
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

The cold hits, your dog hoovers his bowl faster than usual, and suddenly you are wondering: should I be feeding him more? It is a fair question. And the honest answer is: it depends, but probably not.

Here is what actually matters in winter dog feeding, and it has nothing to do with what the thermometer says.

The myth worth busting first

Most pet dogs in the US spend the majority of their winter indoors, in a heated home, doing roughly the same things they do in July. A dog that is warm, resting, and going on shorter walks because it is freezing outside is not burning extra calories to stay alive. He does not need extra food. He might actually need a little less.

The "dogs need more food in winter" idea comes from a real place. Dogs and other mammals that live outside in cold temperatures do burn more calories maintaining body heat. Working dogs, hunting dogs, sled dogs, and dogs that spend significant time outdoors in genuine winter conditions have higher caloric needs from around October through March. But that is not most family dogs.

Your indoor dog eating a big portion because "it is cold" is likely to just gain weight quietly over winter and hit spring a few pounds heavier than he should be.

So what should guide your winter dog feeding?

Body condition, full stop.

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple, hands-on way to check whether your dog is at a healthy weight. It does not require a scale, a vet visit, or any special tools. You just need your hands and about 30 seconds.

Here is how to do a basic check:

  • Feel the ribs. Run your hands along your dog's ribcage. You should be able to feel each rib with gentle pressure, but not see them prominently from across the room. If you cannot feel them at all without pressing hard, your dog is carrying too much weight. If they are obvious to the eye, he may be too lean.
  • Look from above. A healthy dog has a visible waist, a narrowing between the ribcage and the hips. No waist visible means excess weight.
  • Look from the side. The abdomen should tuck up slightly behind the ribcage, not hang level or droop down.

Most vets use a 1 to 9 scale, where 4 to 5 is ideal. You do not need to memorize the scale, but you do need to do this check regularly. Every two weeks through winter is a solid habit.

If anything looks or feels off, especially if you notice rapid weight gain or loss that does not match a change in feeding or activity, that is a conversation for your vet before you adjust anything.

Signs you might be slightly underfeeding

Your dog is always ravenous well before mealtimes, not just occasionally hopeful. He is losing visible muscle definition. His coat looks dull and his energy is lower than usual. His ribs are easy to see without touching.

Signs you might be overfeeding

No waist visible from above. Ribs require firm pressure to feel. The belly droops when he walks. He is slowing down on walks in a way that feels like more than just winter reluctance.

The exception: active and outdoor dogs

If your dog genuinely works in winter, hunts regularly, spends multiple hours outside in cold temperatures, or keeps a serious exercise schedule regardless of the season, his caloric needs may well be higher from late fall through early spring.

For these dogs, it is not about blindly adding food. It is about monitoring body condition more closely and adjusting in small increments, typically 10 percent or less at a time, then checking condition again after a couple of weeks before adjusting further.

Working dogs in very cold climates can need meaningfully more calories, and their food may need to be higher in fat to support that output. If you are managing a dog in those conditions, your vet or a canine nutritionist is the right person to build a winter feeding plan with.

What about the hungrier behavior?

Dogs are opportunists. If your dog seems to act hungrier in winter, it is worth asking what has actually changed. Less exercise often leads to more restlessness and food-seeking behavior. A dog who is bored and under-stimulated will tell you he is starving all day long, whether it is winter or not.

Before you add to the bowl, consider whether his activity level has genuinely dropped and whether that restlessness is hunger or just boredom.

How to adjust feeding without guessing

Check body condition every two weeks. If condition is ideal, keep feeding the same. If he is gaining, reduce by about 10 percent and recheck in two weeks. If he is losing condition and it is not explained by illness, increase by about 10 percent and recheck. Never make large sudden changes to a dog's diet without your vet's input, particularly if there is a health condition involved.

Use the feeding guidelines on your dog's food as a starting point only. They are broad estimates. Your dog's actual ideal portion depends on his size, age, activity level, and metabolism. Two dogs of the same breed and weight can have noticeably different needs.

When to talk to a vet

Weight changes that happen quickly and do not match any change in food or exercise are worth a vet conversation. The same goes for a dog who loses condition despite eating well, seems lethargic beyond normal winter laziness, or has any health condition that could affect metabolism. Hypothyroidism, Cushing's disease, and other conditions can affect weight and appetite. This post will not help you figure those out, and neither will an extra scoop of kibble. Your vet is the right call.

The bottom line

Winter dog feeding does not need to be complicated. Most indoor dogs need roughly the same food in January as they do in June. Active and outdoor dogs may need a little more. The thermometer outside does not tell you anything useful. Your dog's body does.

Check those ribs. Check that waist. Adjust in small steps. Check again. That is the whole system, and it works year-round. 🐾
