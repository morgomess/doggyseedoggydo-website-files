Raw vs. Kibble vs. Fresh: We Broke Down the Research So You Don't Have To

Mar 10, 2025•9 min read•Dog Nutrition
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

Ask the internet what to feed your dog and you'll get three camps yelling past each other. We read through the actual evidence on raw, kibble, and fresh-cooked diets so you can make a calm, informed decision instead of a panicked one.

First, the only rule that always applies

Whatever you feed, it needs to be complete and balanced for your dog's life stage. In practical terms, that means the food meets the nutrient profiles set by AAFCO (the Association of American Feed Control Officials). A diet can be raw, cooked, or kibble and still be excellent - or any of those three and still be dangerously incomplete. Format is not the same as quality.

Hold onto that, because most of the online shouting treats format as if it were the whole story. It isn't.

Kibble: the convenient default

Dry kibble dominates for real reasons. It's shelf-stable, affordable, easy to portion, and the major brands run feeding trials and employ veterinary nutritionists. A well-formulated kibble is a genuinely solid choice that keeps millions of dogs healthy for full lifespans.

The tradeoffs: kibble is processed at high heat, which is efficient but does alter some nutrients (brands compensate by adding them back). It's also calorie-dense and low in moisture, so portion discipline matters more, and some dogs do better with added water or wet food mixed in. Quality varies enormously between brands, which is exactly why the label matters more than the marketing - more on reading labels in our decoder guide.

Fresh-cooked: the rising middle ground

Fresh diets - gently cooked, refrigerated or frozen, portioned to your dog - have exploded in popularity, and the appeal is obvious. The ingredients look like food. Many dogs find them more palatable, which helps with picky eaters and seniors. Early research on minimally processed diets is promising, particularly around digestibility.

The honest caveats: fresh food costs substantially more, especially for large dogs. Quality depends entirely on whether the company formulates to AAFCO standards with proper supplementation - a homemade chicken-and-rice bowl is not balanced no matter how wholesome it looks, and DIY fresh diets are where most nutritional deficiencies sneak in. If you go fresh, use a company with veterinary nutritionists on staff or work with one directly.

Raw: the most polarizing option

Raw feeding advocates point to ancestral diets and report glossy coats and smaller stools. Some dogs do visibly well on raw. But this is the format where the evidence and the enthusiasm diverge most sharply, and it's worth being clear-eyed.

The documented risks are real: raw meat can carry Salmonella, Listeria, and other pathogens that threaten both the dog and the humans handling the food, especially anyone immunocompromised in the household. Bones, a common component, carry fracture and obstruction risks. And many homemade raw diets are nutritionally unbalanced when analyzed. The "ancestral diet" argument is also shakier than it sounds, since dogs diverged from wolves thousands of years ago and evolved to digest starch alongside humans.

None of that means raw can't be done responsibly. It means raw demands the most knowledge, the most hygiene discipline, and ideally professional formulation. It's the option with the least room for winging it.

So what should you actually do?

Here's the framework we'd use:

Start with your dog, not the trend. A healthy young dog thriving on quality kibble has no medical reason to switch. "Could be better" is doing a lot of work in most upgrade pitches.

Match the diet to real factors. Budget, your dog's health conditions, palatability problems, your time, and your tolerance for food-safety protocols all legitimately shape the right answer. A senior who's stopped eating kibble is a great candidate for fresh. A multi-dog household on a tight budget probably isn't.

Demand formulation, whatever you pick. The question that cuts through the marketing on every brand: "Is this formulated by a veterinary nutritionist to meet AAFCO profiles, and do you run feeding trials?" Strong brands in all three categories answer confidently.

Loop in your vet. Especially for dogs with kidney issues, allergies, weight problems, or other conditions where diet is part of the treatment.

The bottom line

There is no single best format - there's the best execution of a format for your specific dog. A balanced kibble, a properly formulated fresh diet, and a responsibly built raw diet can all produce a healthy dog. A careless version of any of them can't. Spend your energy on formulation quality and portion control, not on winning an internet argument.
