How to Teach the Place Command: Crate-Free Calm That Sticks

Sep 16, 2026•6 min read•Training & Behavior
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

Some dogs hate crates. Some apartments have nowhere to put one. Some owners just want a solution that doesn't involve closing a door and crossing their fingers.

Place training is that solution.

It is not a crate alternative in the sense that it confines your dog. It is better than that. A solid place command means your dog chooses to hold a spot, on an open mat, in the middle of the room, while dinner gets made or the front door opens. No latch required. No anxiety spiral. Just a dog who has learned that their mat is the best spot in the house.

That is a skill worth building.

What place actually is (and what it isn't)

Let's be precise, because this matters for training.

Place is not the same as stay. Stay asks your dog to hold a position wherever they happen to be standing. Place sends your dog to a specific location and asks them to hold it there. The mat is part of the cue. That boundary is what makes it so useful.

Place is not crate training either. Crate training uses confinement. Place uses choice. Your dog can leave. The training builds a reason they don't want to.

Related reading: Crate Training 101: How to Make the Crate Your Dog's Favorite Place, if your dog does well with a crate most of the time.

If your dog is fine with a crate most of the time but struggles in specific situations, both tools can coexist. But for dogs who find crates genuinely distressing, place training offers a positive reinforcement dog training path to the same calm outcome without the enclosed space.

Pick the right mat

The mat is the cue as much as the word is. Choose something with a clear edge your dog can feel underfoot: a raised orthopedic bed, a foam mat, or even a flat rubber-backed rug. The edge matters because your dog needs to understand what "on" and "off" means.

A few things that work well:

  • Something portable so you can generalize later.
  • A surface your dog already gravitates toward. Comfort buys you cooperation.
  • One mat to start, not three scattered around the house.

Avoid anything slippery. A dog who slides when they try to lie down is a dog who will not enjoy the mat.

Build it in stages

This is where most people rush and then wonder why the dog won't hold it. Go slower than feels necessary and you will get there faster.

Stage 1: The mat is worth visiting

Put the mat on the floor. Say nothing. The moment your dog steps on it, mark with a yes or a clicker and reward. Toss the treat off the mat so your dog has to step back on to get another. Repeat until your dog is deliberately targeting the mat. This usually takes one short session, five to ten minutes max.

Stage 2: Add the cue and the down

Once your dog is confidently stepping onto the mat, start saying "place" as they move toward it. Then wait for a lie-down before you mark and reward. If your dog is stalling in a sit, lure the down gently or just wait. The down matters because it signals a physical settling, not just parking.

Stage 3: Duration without nagging

This is where place training earns its reputation. You are not repeating the cue. You are not reminding your dog to stay. You say place once, reward the down, and then start delivering rewards at intervals while your dog holds the position.

Start at five seconds. Build to thirty. Then two minutes. Then five. Use a release word, "free," "okay," or whatever you choose, to end every single rep. The release word is not optional. It tells your dog the job is over and that they were the one holding the behavior until you said so.

The release matters more than people think. Without it, your dog is guessing when they are done. With it, they know that getting up without hearing the word was their choice, not yours.

Stage 4: Real-life distractions

This is the part that actually changes your household. Practice place while you:

  • Walk to the kitchen and start cooking
  • Open and close the front door
  • Have a visitor sit down

Related reading: How to Stop Your Dog Jumping on Guests Without Yelling at Anyone, where a solid place does a lot of the work at the front door.

Add one distraction at a time. Go back to shorter durations when you raise the difficulty. A dog who holds place for five minutes in a quiet room might only manage thirty seconds when someone knocks. That is normal. Build back up.

Why your dog keeps popping off early

Three reasons cover almost every case.

  • The duration is too long. You pushed past what your dog can currently do. Go back a step and reward more frequently at a level they can succeed at.
  • The reward stopped being worth it. If your dog has figured out that leaving the mat is also fine, they will leave. Keep the reward rate high enough that staying is the better deal. A stuffed Kong on the mat or periodic high-value treats will hold a dog a lot longer than intermittent kibble.
  • The release word is fuzzy. If your dog is unsure what ends the behavior, they will self-release. Practice ending cleanly and consistently so your dog understands the word is meaningful.

None of these are character flaws in your dog. They are feedback about where to adjust your training.

Generalizing to other rooms and places

A dog who only holds place in the living room is useful in the living room. A dog who holds place in the kitchen, the backyard, a hotel room, or a friend's house is genuinely life-changing.

Start generalizing early. Bring the mat to a new room in week two. Take it to the backyard. Take it to a calm outdoor cafe. The mat travels with the cue, which is why the portable surface choice at the start matters.

Related reading: Why Your Dog Listens at Home but Ignores You at the Park, which explains why every new location needs its own reps.

What place looks like when it's working

The goal is a dog who, when life gets busy or loud, has a default spot to go to. Not because you locked them there. Because that is where the good things happen and that is where they have learned to settle.

Dinner prep gets easier. Guest arrivals get calmer. That constant underfoot orbit starts to fade because your dog has a job with a clear answer.

That is not magic. It is just good training applied consistently.

If your dog is still working through general anxiety or struggles to settle even with a solid routine in place, talking to your vet or a certified trainer is always a reasonable next step. Place training is a powerful tool, but it is one tool, not a full anxiety plan on its own.

Related reading: Separation Anxiety 101: A Complete Guide for Dog Parents, if settling is hard for your dog even with a routine in place.

The bottom line

Start with the mat. One session. See what your dog does. You will probably be surprised how fast they figure it out. 🐾
