Boarding, a Pet Sitter or Bringing the Dog Along? How to Choose Holiday Care Your Dog Can Actually Handle

Nov 17, 2026•7 min read•Dog Health
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

The holidays are coming. Your bags are not going to pack themselves, your aunt's house is four states away, and your dog is sitting there looking at you with those eyes. You need a plan, and you need it before every decent sitter in your zip code gets booked solid.

Here is the honest truth: there is no single best answer for holiday dog care. There is only the right answer for your specific dog. So instead of choosing by price or convenience, let's choose by dog.

Who each option actually suits

Dog boarding facilities

A boarding facility works best for a confident, socialized adult dog who has stayed somewhere unfamiliar before and bounced back without drama. If your dog genuinely likes other dogs, tolerates noise, and settles in a crate or kennel run, a good facility can be a low-stress choice.

The stress cost: group noise, unfamiliar smells, and an unpredictable schedule. For a chill dog, that is a minor inconvenience. For an anxious one, it compounds fast.

Related reading: Crate Training 101: How to Make the Crate Your Dog's Favorite Place.

The one question to ask before booking: "How do you handle a dog who is not eating or showing signs of distress after the first 24 hours, and who do you contact?"

An in-home pet sitter

An in-home sitter stays at your house or makes long visits daily. This option is the gold standard for senior dogs, dogs on daily medication, dogs with separation anxiety, and reactive dogs who do not do well around other animals or strangers. Your dog stays in their own space, on their own schedule, with their own smells. That matters more than most people realize.

Related reading: Aging Gracefully: How to Support Your Senior Dog's Quality of Life.

Related reading: Separation Anxiety 101: A Complete Guide for Dog Parents.

The stress cost: the sitter is a new person in a familiar space. Some dogs warm up fast. Others need a meet-and-greet first, which any good sitter will offer.

The one question to ask before booking: "Have you cared for a dog with similar needs before, and can I speak to that client?"

Drop-in visits

Drop-ins work for independent dogs who are fine alone for stretches of time and just need someone to handle feeding, a potty break, and a bit of company. They are not suitable for dogs with serious separation anxiety or medical needs that require close monitoring. Puppies usually need more coverage than drop-ins can offer.

The stress cost: long stretches alone with brief human contact. Fine for the right dog. Not fine for a velcro breed left alone ten hours a day.

The one question to ask before booking: "How long will each visit actually be, and what happens if you cannot make a scheduled visit?"

Bringing the dog to relatives or traveling together

If you are driving, your dog is comfortable in the car, the destination is dog-friendly, and the people there actually want the dog around, bringing your dog along can work well. It keeps your routine together and eliminates the care coordination entirely.

Related reading: Road Trips With Your Dog: Car Safety, Carsickness, and Long-Drive Survival.

The stress cost: a new environment, new people, possibly new animals, disrupted sleep, and a lot of excited children who do not know that petting a dog while it eats is a bad idea. Great dogs can still have hard visits when the environment is chaotic.

The one question to ask before you go: "Is every person at the destination genuinely okay with a dog being there, and does the dog have a quiet space to decompress?"

A note on puppies: a young puppy does not belong in a facility with unknown dogs and limited one-on-one attention. A dedicated, experienced in-home sitter is almost always the better call.

How to vet a sitter or facility properly

This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that matters most.

References: ask for two or three, and actually call them. Ask if they would use this sitter or facility again and if anything went wrong during the stay.

Insurance and credentials: a professional sitter should carry pet sitter insurance. Facilities should be licensed according to your state's requirements. Ask directly. A good one will not hesitate.

A visit first: before any booking is confirmed, go in person. Watch how the staff or sitter interacts with dogs. Look at the space. Trust your gut and your dog's reaction.

Vaccine requirements: any reputable boarding facility will require current rabies, distemper, and bordetella vaccines at minimum. If a facility does not ask for proof, that is a red flag.

Emergency protocols: ask who makes the call if your dog needs a vet, whether they have a vet on call or a clinic they use, and whether they will contact you before authorizing non-emergency treatment. Get the answers in writing.

What to pack and write down

Whether your dog is staying home with a sitter or going somewhere new, leave a clear written document. Not a text message. An actual document.

  • The feeding amount per meal and the schedule, not just the brand.
  • Every medication with the dose, the timing, and what it looks like, so there is no confusion.
  • Your vet's name, clinic address, and phone number.
  • An emergency contact who is not you, in case you are on a plane.

Include the one cue your dog knows reliably. If your dog settles on "place," write that down. If they know "wait" before crossing a threshold, that is worth noting. A sitter who knows your dog's language is a better sitter.

Pack enough of your dog's regular food for the full stay plus a few days extra. A change in diet mid-stay adds digestive stress you do not need. Include a worn item of clothing with your scent if your dog finds that comforting.

One thing most owners forget: write down what normal looks like for your dog. If your dog usually skips breakfast when excited or drinks a lot of water after a walk, say that. It helps a sitter distinguish quirks from warning signs.

Book now, before the slots are gone

Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26, and December fills up fast after that. Good in-home sitters in most US cities have been taking holiday bookings for weeks, and many are already full. Quality boarding facilities with small group sizes or private runs go first.

If you are still figuring out which option fits your dog, that is fair. But make the decision this week, not the week before you leave. Your dog deserves a plan that actually suits them, not a last-minute scramble that suits your schedule. 🐾
