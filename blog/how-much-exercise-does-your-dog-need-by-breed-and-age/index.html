How Much Exercise Does Your Dog Really Need?

Jul 19, 2026•7 min read•Health
🐾By the Doggy See, Doggy Do Editorial Team
How we research and review our content

You got a dog. You walk them. Job done, right?

Not quite. Exercise for dogs is not a one-size-fits-all situation, and a Border Collie on a 20-minute stroll is just as poorly served as a Bulldog being pushed through a three-mile run. Getting this wrong shows up in chewed furniture, anxious behavior, sore joints, and a dog who is either wired or wiped out for the wrong reasons.

Here is what your dog actually needs, by breed type and life stage, plus how to spot when you are over- or under-doing it.

Why Breed Type Matters More Than You Think

Dog breeds were developed for specific jobs. That history is still running in their biology, and ignoring it leads to frustrated dogs and confused owners.

Working and herding breeds - think Siberian Huskies, Border Collies, Australian Shepherds, and Belgian Malinois - were built to go hard, all day. These dogs generally need 1.5 to 2 hours of vigorous activity daily, and mental stimulation on top of that. A walk around the block is a warmup, not a workout.

Sporting breeds like Labradors, Golden Retrievers, Spaniels, and Pointers sit in a similar bracket. They thrive on an hour or more of active exercise each day. Swimming, fetch, trail running - these dogs are genuinely happiest when they have something physical to do with you.

Terriers are small, but do not let that fool you. Jack Russells, Airedales, and similar breeds were bred to hunt and dig with serious tenacity. They need a solid 45 to 60 minutes daily, ideally with variety to keep their sharp minds engaged.

Toy breeds - Chihuahuas, Maltese, Shih Tzus - have real energy but smaller bodies to match. Around 20 to 30 minutes of daily activity is generally enough, split into shorter sessions. These dogs tire faster and can overheat more easily.

Flat-faced (brachycephalic) breeds deserve a special mention. Pugs, French Bulldogs, Bulldogs, and Boston Terriers have shortened airways that make breathing harder during exercise. They need activity, but low intensity, in cool conditions, with close attention to any signs of labored breathing. Short, gentle walks and play sessions work far better than sustained exertion for these dogs.

Age Changes Everything

A dog's exercise needs shift significantly at each life stage, and matching your routine to where your dog actually is right now is one of the most useful things you can do for them.

Puppies

Puppies have energy in bursts, but their bones and joints are still developing. A commonly referenced guideline is around five minutes of structured exercise per month of age, up to twice daily. So a four-month-old puppy is looking at 20-minute sessions, not an hour-long run. Too much high-impact exercise too early can put real strain on developing growth plates.

Free play in a safe space is great at this age. Keep walks shorter and let the puppy set some of the pace.

Adult Dogs

This is the stage where breed-appropriate activity really kicks in. Most adult dogs need daily exercise that genuinely challenges them, not just a walk that gets them outside and back. What that looks like depends entirely on breed type, as above, but the principle is consistent: regular, varied, and appropriately demanding.

Mental stimulation matters here too. A tired brain is just as important as a tired body, especially for intelligent breeds.

Senior Dogs

Older dogs slow down, but they do not stop needing movement. Gentle, regular walks help maintain joint mobility, healthy weight, and mental engagement. The distance and intensity should reduce, not the consistency.

Watch for signs of stiffness after exercise, reluctance to move, or lagging on walks that used to be easy. These can be worth a conversation with your vet about adjusting the routine or addressing joint comfort.

Signs You Are Getting It Wrong

Under-exercised dogs tend to make their feelings known. Destructive behavior, excessive barking, difficulty settling, jumping, and general chaos around the house are all classic signs of a dog with nowhere to put their energy.

Over-exercised dogs can be harder to spot, because we tend to assume more exercise is always better. Watch for excessive panting that takes a long time to settle, limping or stiffness after walks, reluctance to go out, or paw pads that are sore or worn. Young dogs and seniors are most vulnerable here.

The goal is a dog who is pleasantly tired after activity, settles well, and is engaged and happy between sessions. Not one who is either bouncing off walls or dragging themselves around.

Mental Stimulation Is Not Optional

Physical exercise handles the body. Mental enrichment handles the brain. For high-drive breeds especially, ignoring mental stimulation is like solving half the equation.

  • Sniff work and nose games. Hide food or a toy and let your dog use their nose to find it. This is deeply tiring for dogs in a way that walking simply is not.
  • Food puzzles and Kongs. A stuffed Kong or puzzle feeder turns mealtime into a proper job. Worth ten minutes of physical exercise, easy.
  • Training sessions. Short, positive training sessions, even just 10 minutes, work your dog's brain hard and strengthen your communication with them.
  • Chew time. Appropriate chewing is genuinely calming for most dogs. A good chew gives them something satisfying to focus on.

Adapting When the Weather or Circumstances Get in the Way

Bad weather, injury, or a particularly hectic week happen to everyone. When outdoor exercise is not possible or needs to be reduced, leaning harder on indoor enrichment makes a real difference.

Scatter feeding in the yard or across a sniff mat. Indoor agility with cushions and chairs. Hide-and-seek with you or their favorite toy. Training new skills. Chew sessions. These are not second-best options; they are legitimate, effective ways to tire your dog out when the usual routine is off the table.

For dogs with health conditions or ongoing mobility issues, your vet or a canine physical therapist can help you design a routine that keeps them active safely.

The Bottom Line

Dog exercise is not just about hitting a daily step count. It is about matching what you do to who your dog actually is: their breed history, their age, and their individual health. Get that match right, and you have a dog who is calmer, happier, and genuinely easier to live with.

Start by looking at your dog's breed group and life stage. Build from there. And if your dog is telling you something is off, through behavior, energy levels, or reluctance, listen to them. They are usually right. 🐶
